You have been making changes. More eggs in the morning. Fewer carbs at night. A walk after dinner. Cutting out the thing someone told you was the problem.

And it feels like progress, right up until you notice something uncomfortable. You have never once been certain any of it would work. You have been doing things that sound healthy and hoping the sum of them adds up.

That is the actual problem. Not effort. You have plenty of effort. You have never had a shortage of that in any other part of your life.

The problem is that nobody ever told you which things count.

## Part one. Why nothing has ever felt certain

Moving abroad handed you a second road to walk, and this one was never mapped for you. So you did what anyone would do. You wandered. You tried a thing for six weeks, saw nothing, decided it was wrong, and tried something else.

And you are smart, which made it worse. Every expert says something different. Bulk or cut. High carb or low carb. Push pull legs or upper lower. You researched for months and switched approaches every few weeks, and you spent more mental energy on fitness decisions than you ever spent actually training.

  ![The Paved Path](/images/the-paved-path.png "The Paved Path")

Both of those paths reach the same place. One of them takes three years and costs you a piece of yourself every time it breaks. The other is short because there is no wandering in it.

Fitness does not reward analysis. It rewards execution. Twelve weeks of average programming executed consistently beats perfect programming attempted inconsistently, every single time.

## Part two. The four levers

This is the part that annoys people when they first hear it, because they have spent years believing fitness is complicated.

<Frame>
  ![Four Levers](/images/four-levers.png "Four Levers")
</Frame>

Calories decide whether the weight on the scale moves at all. Protein decides whether that movement is muscle or not. Training tells your body which tissue to keep and which to spend. Overload is what makes any of it continue past the first month.

Every other question you have ever asked about fitness is downstream of these four, and most of those questions do not matter at all.

Get these right and the outcome stops being a hope. It becomes arithmetic.

## Part three. Nutrition

Think of fitness like opening a very heavy door. Training is the key. Nutrition is you pushing the door open. The key is useless on its own, and roughly seventy percent of your result comes from this section rather than the next one.

### Your calorie number

Your body runs on two numbers. Maintenance, which is what it burns in a day. And consumed, which is what you eat in a day.

Eat less than maintenance and you lose weight. Eat more and you gain. Eat the same and you stay. There is no fourth possibility, and no food is exempt from this.

<Frame>
  ![Calorie Math](/images/calorie-math.png "Calorie Math")
</Frame>

<Steps>
  <Step title="Convert your weight to pounds">
    Weight in kilograms, multiplied by 2.2.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply by the number for your goal">
    Losing weight is 10, 11 or 12. Maintaining is 13, 14 or 15. Gaining muscle is 16, 17 or 18.
  </Step>
</Steps>

An 80kg person who wants to gain muscle: 80 times 2.2 is 176 pounds. 176 times 17 is 2,992 calories a day.

Pick the middle number in your range to start. You will adjust it later based on what actually happens on the scale, not on what a calculator predicted.

<Note>
  This number is an estimate and it is supposed to be. Nobody's true maintenance is knowable on paper. You use it as a starting point, run it for two weeks, and let the weekly average tell you whether it was too high or too low. Part eight covers exactly how.
</Note>

### Your protein number

Calories decide whether the number on the scale moves. Protein decides whether that movement is muscle or not.

This is the entire difference between losing weight and changing how you look. Two people can lose the same ten kilos and end up in completely different bodies, and protein is most of why.

<Frame>
  ![Protein First](/images/protein-first.png "Protein First")
</Frame>

Your weight in pounds, times one, in grams. 176 pounds is 176 grams a day. There is no second step, no complicated formula, and no PhD required.

Then think about your calories the way you would think about pocket money. You buy your protein first, every single day, before anything else. Once that is done, whatever calories are left are yours to spend on whatever you actually want. It genuinely does not matter what.

That last part is not a concession to make this easier. It is the reason this survives past week three.

### How to track it

Pick one of these. Not all three.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="MyFitnessPal, recommended">
    Search any food, scan barcodes, and it logs everything automatically. The free version is more than enough. This is the lowest friction option and the one most people should use.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="ChatGPT, if you hate apps">
    Tell it what you ate as you go and let it keep the running total. Start a fresh chat each morning and paste this in first:

    ```text
    You are an expert nutritionist. I will give you food items as I eat
    them during the day. Your job is simple, just give me the Total
    calories and macros of the food I'm eating. Maintain one table where
    you keep appending the food items as I give them to you and have a
    totals display too. Do not add anything else to the response.
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="A gram scale, optional but it is the pro move">
    One banana can be 100g or 200g and the app cannot tell the difference. Thirty seconds per meal removes the guesswork entirely. The most consistent clients all weigh their food.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Part four. How training actually works

Before the program, four things that explain why it is built the way it is. If you understand these, you will not be tempted to change it.

### Your body does not want to build muscle

Your body loves to stay where it is. It does not want to carry more muscle than it needs to survive, because muscle is expensive to maintain. So if you do not have muscle right now, do not hate your body for it. It simply does not believe you need any, and it is conserving resources.

Your job is to make it believe otherwise. You do that by using your muscles beyond what they are currently capable of, so your body concludes that the current amount is not enough and builds more so it does not get caught out again.

In plainer terms: you break the muscle, your body repairs it slightly stronger than before, and then you go and break it again.

### What failure actually means

Every working set in this program goes to failure. Not "kinda hard". Not "pretty tired". Failure, as in you physically cannot complete another rep with proper form.

Four days a week means fewer total sets than most programs. Each one has to count. Half effort across four days is four days of nothing. Going to genuine failure is what extracts every drop of growth from a small number of sets, and it is the entire reason four days is enough.

<Warning>
  Two exceptions on safety, not on effort. On heavy barbell squats, bench press and deadlifts, stop at the point where your form breaks down rather than the point where the bar pins you. Use a spotter or safety pins if you have them. Form failure is the real limit on those three, and it arrives slightly before muscular failure.
</Warning>

### Progressive overload

This is the one almost nobody is told, and it is the reason people train for a year and look identical at the end of it.

If the demand never rises, there is nothing for your body to adapt to. So every week has to ask slightly more than the last one did. One more rep at the same weight, or the same reps at slightly more weight.

Write every set down. Not for discipline reasons. Because you cannot beat last week if you cannot remember what last week was.

### Why four days

Each muscle group gets trained twice a week, which is the sweet spot for growth. Two of your days are built on compound movements where you move the most weight across the biggest muscles. The other two are isolation days where you go after specific muscles directly.

Four sessions also survives a real life. Six days a week collapses the first time your calendar gets serious, and a program you abandon in week five produces nothing.

## Part five. The twelve week program

Four day upper and lower split. Compound days for maximum weight, isolation days for targeted work.

<Frame>
  ![The Training Week](/images/the-training-week.png "The Training Week")
</Frame>

The specific days are not sacred. What matters is the pattern and keeping at least one rest day between sessions. Upper, lower, rest, upper, lower, rest, rest. A week that runs Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday, Saturday is exactly the same program.

Every session should take 45 to 60 minutes.

### Day one. Upper body A, compound focus

Heavy multi joint movements. This day is about moving maximum weight.

| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Barbell Bench Press | 4 to failure | 6 to 12 | 3 min | Chest, front delts, triceps |
| 2 | Barbell Row | 4 to failure | 6 to 12 | 3 min | Lats, mid back, biceps |
| 3 | Overhead Press | 4 to failure | 8 to 12 | 2 to 3 min | Shoulders, triceps |
| 4 | Incline Dumbbell Press | 3 to failure | 8 to 12 | 2 min | Upper chest, front delts |
| 5 | Lat Pulldown or Pull ups | 3 to failure | 8 to 15 | 2 min | Lats, biceps |
| 6 | Barbell Curls | 3 to failure | 8 to 12 | 90 sec | Biceps |
| 7 | Tricep Pushdowns | 3 to failure | 10 to 15 | 90 sec | Triceps |

### Day two. Lower body A, compound focus

Heavy lower body work. Maximum weight, maximum growth.

| # | Exercise | Sets | Reps | Rest | Muscles |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |

| 2 | Romanian Deadlift | 4 to failure | 8 to 12 | 3 min | Hamstrings, glutes, lower back |

| 5 | Bulgarian Split Squats | 3 to failure each leg | 8 to 12 | 90 sec between legs | Quads, glutes |

## Part six. Progression across the twelve weeks

Progressive overload is the only reason you get stronger over time. Here is how it plays out across the block.

<Frame>
  ![Twelve Week Progression](/images/twelve-week-progression.png "Twelve Week Progression")
</Frame>

| Phase | What you are doing |
| :-- | :-- |
| Weeks 1 to 2 | Learn the movements. Find the weight that brings you to failure inside the rep range. |
| Weeks 3 to 6 | Push for one or two more reps each week on the main lifts. Weight stays the same. |
| Weeks 7 to 10 | Once you hit the top of the rep range, increase the weight by 2.5 to 5kg and start again at the bottom. |
| Weeks 11 to 12 | Deload. Reduce weight by 30 percent and focus purely on form. |

A worked example on bench press. Week 3 you press 100kg for 8 reps. By week 6 you are aiming for 100kg for 10. From week 7 you move to 105kg for 6 to 8 reps and start climbing again.

The deload at the end is not a rest week you have earned. It is part of the programming. Growth catches up during it.

## Part seven. What you can stop thinking about

Here is the part that should be a relief. Almost everything you have quietly worried about was never deciding anything.

<Frame>
  ![Signal And Noise](/images/signal-and-noise.png "Signal And Noise")
</Frame>

Carbs at night, fasted cardio, the perfect exercise selection, whether your split is theoretically optimal, the supplement someone swore by. None of it will move you if the four numbers are wrong, and none of it will stop you if they are right.

The reason this matters is not that the details are harmful. It is that you have a finite amount of attention and you have been spending it in the wrong place.

## Part eight. How you know it is working

Two weeks. Same calories, same protein, four sessions.

Weigh yourself at the same time on the same days and take the weekly average. Never react to a single morning, because daily weight moves with water, salt, sleep and what you ate the night before, none of which is fat.

| What the average did after two weeks | What you do |
| :-- | :-- |
| Moving the way you want, roughly 0.25 to 1 percent of bodyweight per week | Nothing. The number was right. Leave it alone. |
| Not moving at all, and you actually hit your numbers | Adjust calories by around 200 in the direction you need and run another two weeks. |
| Not moving, and you know you missed days | Do not touch the number. Run it properly for two weeks first. |

That is the whole feedback loop. You are not guessing anymore. You have a number, a result, and a correction.

## Part nine. Questions you are going to have

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="What if I miss a workout?">
    Do it the next day. Do not skip it. Shift everything by one day and continue. Life happens, and twelve weeks of consistency matters far more than any single session.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can I add more exercises?">
    No. More is not better. This is already the right amount of work for four days. Adding exercises means you cannot give one hundred percent to the things that matter. Stick to the plan.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="What about abs?">
    Every compound movement in here trains your core. If you want direct work, add three sets of planks at the end of your upper days. But visible abs are a nutrition outcome, not a training one.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="My gym does not have the equipment">
    Use the closest alternative. No barbell, use dumbbells. No cable machine, use bands. The movement pattern matters much more than the specific tool.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Can I switch the days around?">
    Yes. Keep the pattern: compound upper, compound lower, rest, isolation upper, isolation lower, rest, rest. The specific days do not matter at all. The pattern does.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="I am not seeing results">
    It is almost always one of three things. You are not actually going to failure on every set. Your nutrition is off, usually protein. Or you have not been consistent for a full twelve weeks yet. Most people quit around week six.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
