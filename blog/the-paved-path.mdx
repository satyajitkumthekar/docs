You have been making changes. More eggs in the morning. Fewer carbs at night. A walk after dinner. Cutting out the thing someone told you was the problem.

And it feels like progress, right up until you notice something uncomfortable. You have never once been certain any of it would work. You have been doing things that sound healthy and hoping the sum of them adds up.

That is the actual problem. Not effort. You have plenty of effort. You have never had a shortage of that in any other part of your life.

The problem is that nobody ever told you which things count.

## The road is long enough already

Moving abroad handed you a second road to walk, and this one was never mapped for you. So you did what anyone would do. You wandered. You tried a thing for six weeks, saw nothing, decided it was wrong, and tried something else.

<Frame>
  ![Two ways to cover the same distance](/images/paved-path/the-paved-path.png "Two ways to cover the same distance")
</Frame>

Both of those paths reach the same place. One of them takes three years and costs you a piece of yourself every time it breaks. The other is short because there is no wandering in it.

This document is the second one. It is not motivation and it is not a challenge. It is a map, so that from here on you are executing rather than guessing.

## Everything is four numbers

This is the part that annoys people when they first hear it, because they have spent years believing fitness is complicated.

<Frame>
  ![Everything that decides your body is four numbers](/images/paved-path/four-levers.png "Everything that decides your body is four numbers")
</Frame>

Four. Calories, protein, training, overload. Every other question you have ever asked about fitness is downstream of these, and most of them do not matter at all.

Get these four right and the outcome stops being a hope. It becomes arithmetic.

## One. Your calories

Your body runs on two numbers. What it burns in a day, and what you eat in a day.

Eat less than you burn and you lose weight. Eat more and you gain. Eat the same and you stay. There is no fifth possibility, and no food is exempt from this.

<Frame>
  ![Your calorie number](/images/paved-path/calorie-math.png "Your calorie number")
</Frame>

Take your bodyweight in kilos, multiply by 2.2 to get pounds, then multiply that by the number for your goal. Losing fat is 10 to 12. Maintaining is 13 to 15. Building muscle is 16 to 18.

Pick the middle number in your range. You will adjust it later based on what actually happens, not on what a calculator predicted.

<Note>
  This number is an estimate, and it is supposed to be. Nobody's true maintenance is knowable on paper. You use it as a starting point, run it for two weeks, and let the scale tell you whether it was too high or too low.
</Note>

## Two. Your protein

Calories decide whether the number on the scale moves. Protein decides whether that movement is muscle or not.

This is the entire difference between losing weight and changing how you look. Two people can lose the same ten kilos and end up in completely different bodies, and protein is most of why.

<Frame>
  ![Protein is bought first](/images/paved-path/protein-first.png "Protein is bought first")
</Frame>

Your weight in pounds, times one, in grams. 176 pounds is 176 grams a day. There is no second step and no formula worth memorising beyond that.

Then think about your calories the way you would think about pocket money. You buy your protein first, every single day, before anything else. Whatever calories are left over are yours to spend on whatever you actually enjoy eating.

That last part is not a concession. It is the reason this survives past week three.

## Three. Your training week

Four sessions a week. Upper body, lower body, upper body, lower body.

Two of those days are built on compound movements, where you are moving the most weight you can across the biggest muscles. The other two are isolation days, where you go after specific muscles directly. Every muscle group gets trained twice a week, with enough space in between to actually recover.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Monday, Upper A">
    Compound focus. Bench, rows, overhead press. The heaviest work you do all week.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Tuesday, Lower A">
    Compound focus. Squats and hinges, the movements that ask the most of you.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Thursday, Upper B">
    Isolation focus. Chest, back, shoulders and arms worked directly.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Friday, Lower B">
    Isolation focus. Quads, hamstrings, glutes and calves worked directly.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The days themselves are not sacred. What matters is the pattern and the recovery gap. If your week runs Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday, Saturday, that is the same program.

Four days is deliberately fewer sessions than you might expect. Which means every set has to be worth something. Taking a set close to failure, where you genuinely could not get another clean rep, is what makes four days enough. Half-effort sets across four days is just four days of nothing.

## Four. Overload

This is the one almost nobody is told, and it is the reason people train for a year and look the same at the end of it.

Your body does not build muscle because you went to the gym. It builds muscle because something asked more of it than it could comfortably handle, so it adapts to be ready next time. If the demand never rises, there is nothing to adapt to.

So the job across a week is simple. Do a little more than last time. One more rep in the same set, or the same reps with slightly more weight. When you hit the top of your rep range on a movement, add weight and start again at the bottom of the range.

Write it down. Not for discipline reasons. Because you cannot beat last week if you cannot remember what last week was.

## What you can stop thinking about

Here is the part that should be a relief. Almost everything you have quietly worried about was never deciding anything.

<Frame>
  ![What decides it, and what never did](/images/paved-path/signal-and-noise.png "What decides it, and what never did")
</Frame>

Carbs at night, fasted cardio, the perfect exercise selection, whether your split is theoretically optimal, the supplement someone swore by. None of it is going to move you if the four numbers are wrong, and none of it is going to stop you if they are right.

The reason this matters is not that the details are harmful. It is that you have a finite amount of attention and you have been spending it in the wrong place.

## How you know it is working

Two weeks. Same calories, same protein, four sessions, weigh yourself at the same time on the same days and take the weekly average rather than reacting to any single morning.

If the average is moving in the direction you want at somewhere between a quarter and one percent of your bodyweight a week, the number was right. Leave it alone.

If nothing has moved after two full weeks of actually hitting your numbers, adjust calories by around 200 and run it another two weeks.

That is the whole feedback loop. You are not guessing anymore. You have a number, a result, and a correction.

## The honest part

You now have the map. That is genuinely the easy half, and I want to be straight with you about that rather than pretend this document solves everything.

Knowing what to do has never been what stopped you. If information were the barrier, you would have solved this years ago, because you are clearly capable of learning things.

What stops people is the other two roads. Making this fit a life that is already full, so that a travel week or a brutal quarter does not end the whole thing. And holding yourself to it on the days nobody is watching, which is the one thing you were never wired for.

Those are the next two parts, and they are where this actually gets won or lost.
