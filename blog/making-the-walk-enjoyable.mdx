You have a very specific image of what a fitness routine is supposed to look like.

Six in the morning, in the gym. Six days a week. Ninety minute sessions. The perfect diet running alongside it. Every week, exactly the same.

Social media drilled it in. Movies reinforced it. And now that image sits in your head as the definition of what a real routine looks like.

And because your actual life never matches that picture, you tell yourself it is not the right time.

So you wait. You wait for the calm season, for the project to wrap, for the travel to slow down, for life to settle. That window where everything aligns barely ever comes, and when it does it lasts two weeks before something else takes over.

Meanwhile the person who stopped waiting and started adapting is three years in.

## Part one. You are not failing a routine

You are failing an image of a routine, which is a different thing.

<Frame>
  ![Picture Vs Reality](/images/picture-vs-reality.png "Picture Vs Reality")
</Frame>

This is not about lowering the bar. It is about being honest about what your actual life requires and then building something that survives it.

My own training has not looked the same once in three years. Weeks of travel. A startup scaling from zero to a unicorn. Moving cities. Family. The routine changed constantly. The commitment never did.

<Note>
  **The shift.** Stop trying to build a routine that survives your ideal life. Build one that survives your actual life.
</Note>

The difference between people who stay in shape for years and people who cycle in and out is not discipline, and it is not willpower, and it is not even motivation. It is the ability to recognise which season they are in and match the approach to that season instead of forcing a mismatch.

## Part two. Find your season

Look at your life honestly. Not the version you want, the version you are actually living this week.

<Frame>
  ![Four Seasons](/images/four-seasons.png "Four Seasons")
</Frame>

|  | Super busy | Chaos | Moderate | Light |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **When you are in it** | Travel, crisis, deadlines. No gym access. Zero predictability. | 60 plus hour weeks. Unpredictable calendar. Barely time to think. | 40 to 50 hour weeks. Mostly predictable. Manageable chaos. | Light schedule. Sleep dialled in. Energy high. Life aligned. |
| **Your training** | 3 home sessions a week, or 2 home plus 1 full body | Full body, twice a week, any 2 days | Upper, lower, upper, lower. The full 4 day split | Upper, lower, run. Upper, lower, run. One rest day |
| **Your nutrition** | Hit protein. Move as much as possible. | Hit protein. Hit 8,000 steps. Everything else free. | Anchor meal locked in. Calories roughly tracked. | Full tracking. Protein dialled. Fully intentional. |
| **The goal** | Retain muscle. Keep the habit alive. | Protect what you have built. | Maintain. Steady progress. | Grow. Maximum adaptation. |

Your season can change week to week. That is the entire point. The week reshapes the moment the season does, rather than two weeks after you have already missed everything and decided you have fallen off.

## Part three. One program, four shapes

This is the part that keeps the whole thing simple, so it is worth being explicit about it.

You are not switching programs when your season changes. Every gym session in every season is built out of the same lifts you already know from The Paved Path. What changes is the shape of the week, not the movements in it.

<Frame>
  ![Season Ladder](/images/season-ladder.png "Season Ladder")
</Frame>

That matters more than it sounds. Every time a plan asks you to learn a genuinely new program, you pay a tax in decisions, in confidence, and in the two weeks it takes to work out your weights again. You should never pay that tax. You learn the lifts once and after that you are only choosing the shape of the week.

### Chaos. Full body, twice a week

Any two days. Five lifts, all of them already in your block.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps | Rest |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Barbell Squat | 3 to failure | 6 to 12 | 3 min |
| Barbell Bench Press | 3 to failure | 6 to 12 | 3 min |
| Barbell Row | 3 to failure | 6 to 12 | 3 min |
| Romanian Deadlift | 3 to failure | 8 to 12 | 3 min |
| Overhead Press | 2 to failure | 8 to 12 | 2 min |

Roughly fifty minutes. Two of these a week means every major muscle still gets trained twice, which is the same frequency you were getting on the four day split. That is why full body beats picking two days off the split when you only have two sessions.

Arms get plenty of indirect work from the pressing and rowing. Do not add curls. You are protecting here, not building.

### Moderate. Upper, lower, upper, lower

The full four day block, exactly as written in The Paved Path. Most of your year should look like this, and this is where actual progress happens.

### Light. Upper, lower, run, upper, lower, run, rest

When a genuinely good season arrives, the instinct is to go find a six day lifting program. Do not.

| Day | Session |
| :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Upper A |
| 2 | Lower A |
| 3 | Run |
| 4 | Upper B |
| 5 | Lower B |
| 6 | Run |
| 7 | Rest |

Six active days, four of them lifting, and the runs sitting between each pair so your lifting days still get separation. Twenty to thirty minutes at conversational pace is all a run needs to be.

The runs are not fat loss work and they are not a requirement. They are conditioning, they are good for your heart and your sleep, and they give you more calories to spend on food you enjoy.

<Warning>
  If you want more than this, add **one set** to the first two lifts of each day. Do not add exercises. The Paved Path is right about that. More movements means less effort per movement and a longer session you will eventually resent.
</Warning>

### Super busy. Three home sessions

This is the one season that genuinely looks different, because you have no gym and no equipment. There is no way to scale a barbell program into a hotel room.

Three of these a week, roughly every other day, each under thirty minutes.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps | Rest |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Push ups, feet elevated if too easy | 3 to failure | Max reps | 60 sec |
| Pike Push ups | 3 to failure | Max reps | 60 sec |
| Bodyweight Squats or Jump Squats | 3 to failure | Max reps | 60 sec |
| Reverse Lunges, each leg | 3 to failure | 12 to 20 each | 60 sec |
| Glute Bridges or Single Leg Glute Bridge | 3 to failure | Max reps | 45 sec |
| Diamond Push ups | 2 to failure | Max reps | 60 sec |
| Plank Hold | 2 | Max time | 45 sec |

Every set still goes to failure. Short on time does not mean short on effort.

**If you get gym access even once that week,** swap one of the three for the full body session above. One session with real weight is worth more than the two bodyweight sessions it replaces, so take it whenever the week offers it.

<Tip>
  **If you have a resistance band,** add band pull aparts and banded rows at the end. Ten extra minutes, and a large difference for your back and rear delts on travel weeks where everything else is pushing.
</Tip>

This session exists for one reason. To keep you in the game when everything else has fallen apart. It does not need to be perfect. It needs to happen.

### What you actually lose when you drop down

This is the fear that makes people quit rather than downshift, so let me address it directly.

Dropping from four days to two for a few weeks costs you very little. Muscle is far harder to lose than it is to build, and it hangs around for weeks on minimal stimulus. Two hard full body sessions a week is comfortably enough to maintain what you have. Even a full week off does almost nothing.

What you will notice is a slight drop in the weight you can move when you come back, and that returns within a session or two. It feels like loss. It is mostly rust.

What actually costs you is the spiral. Two weeks of nothing becomes a month, the month becomes a story about how you fell off again, and the restart begins from a lower place than the last one. That is the thing the season model is built to prevent. Downshifting is not falling off. It is the plan.

## Part four. Making it require no thinking

The reason most routines die is not the training. It is the number of decisions the routine asks you to make on days when you have none left.

So you make all of them once, on Sunday.

<Frame>
  ![Sunday Ritual](/images/sunday-ritual.png "Sunday Ritual")
</Frame>

<Steps>
  <Step title="Be honest about the week">
    Look at what is actually on the calendar, not what you hope it will be. Which season are you in?
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set the weekly target">
    Three home sessions if you are travelling. Two if it is chaos. Four if it is moderate. Four plus two runs if everything is aligned.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find your gaps and block them">
    Look at the week, find your openings, mornings or lunches or evenings, it genuinely does not matter which, and block them like CEO meetings.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Forget about it until the block hits">
    No daily decisions. No daily negotiation with yourself. You show up and execute.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**The anchor workout.** Pick one session that happens no matter how bad the week gets. Mine was always Saturday morning. Build the chaos around it rather than the other way around, because a week where everything collapsed except one session is still a week you did not fall off.

**When a block gets destroyed.** It will. A meeting lands on it, a flight moves, something breaks. The rule is that you move it inside the same week rather than skipping it, and you decide where it moves the moment it gets taken, not later. Later is where sessions go to die. If the week has genuinely closed up, you are not in the season you thought you were in on Sunday, so drop a rung and do the smaller version instead of doing nothing.

The goal here is zero daily decisions about your fitness. You made all of them on Sunday. Now you just execute.

## Part five. Food you would eat anyway

This is the part most programs get wrong, and it is the part that decides whether you are still doing this in a year.

For us, food is a love language. It is how our families showed up for us. I still remember eating my mother's food on a bad day and what that did. Any plan that treats food purely as fuel, that strips out everything you actually love eating, is a plan you will follow for five weeks and then resent.

So the approach is not restriction. It is making one part of your day do all the work, so the rest of it can be whatever you want.

### The anchor meal

Instead of trying to optimise every meal, you fix one meal a day that does all the heavy lifting. Your protein, your nutrients, everything your body actually needs. Lock that in and the rest of your day is free.

<Frame>
  ![Anchor Meal](/images/anchor-meal.png "Anchor Meal")
</Frame>

Every anchor meal starts from the same base. Two scoops of whey, which is 250 calories and 50 grams of protein.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Take your daily calorie target">
    From The Paved Path, your bodyweight in pounds multiplied by the number for your goal.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply it by 0.40">
    That is your anchor meal budget.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Spend the remaining sixty percent however you want">
    No tracking. No stress. No guilt. This is the part that makes the whole thing survivable.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Then pick your anchor meal from the tier that matches your budget.

| Budget | Non veg | Protein | Veg | Protein |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 500 to 600 cal | 2 scoops whey, 150g grilled chicken, big salad | 80g | 2 scoops whey in 300g Greek yoghurt, 200g low-fat cottage cheese, berries | 95g |
| 700 to 800 cal | 2 scoops whey, 200g grilled chicken, 2 whole eggs, salad | 105g | 2 scoops whey in 300g Greek yoghurt, 200g low fat paneer, salad | 110g |
| 900 to 1000 cal | 2 scoops whey in milk, 300g chicken, 3 whole eggs, salad | 140g | 2 scoops whey in milk, 400g firm tofu, 300g Greek yoghurt, salad | 120g |

**Eat it early.** Breakfast or lunch, before the day has a chance to go sideways. The whole point is that your protein is already done before the six o'clock meeting runs long and dinner becomes whatever is nearest. An anchor meal you plan for nine at night is not an anchor.

Notice what this does. Once it is eaten, most people have already hit their protein target for the day. Everything after that is genuinely free.

### Nutrition by season

Same principle as training. Stop trying to eat perfectly every day and match the effort to the season you are in.

| Season | What you actually do |
| :-- | :-- |
| Chaos | Two non negotiables. Hit your protein target and hit 8,000 steps. Everything else is free and you do not stress about any of it. |
| Moderate | Anchor meal locked in. One or two high protein meals doing the heavy lifting daily. The rest of your calories are yours. |
| Light | Fully intentional. Calories tracked, protein dialled, every meal considered. This is your growth window, so make it count. |

### Why steps carry a chaos week

Steps look like the throwaway instruction on that table. They are not, and it is worth understanding why they are the thing you hold onto when the gym goes.

Walking is the only meaningful lever on your daily energy burn that costs you nothing in recovery. Hard training has to be repaid with sleep and food, which is exactly what you do not have in a brutal week. Walking asks for none of that, and across a day it moves more calories than most people expect.

It is also the one thing that stays available when nothing else does. There is no version of your week where you cannot walk.

Practically: take calls walking, get off a stop early, put twenty minutes after dinner in the calendar. Eight thousand is the number because it is genuinely reachable on a bad day, which is the entire point of picking it.

On your hardest weeks the goal is not to eat perfectly. The goal is to not undo everything you have built. Protein and steps keep your muscle, keep your metabolism, and keep the habit alive. On a chaos week that is enough, and treating it as enough is what stops the spiral.

## Part six. Travel, specifically

Travel is the season that breaks most people in this audience, and it deserves its own answer rather than being lumped in with busy.

**Training.** Check whether the hotel has a gym before you assume it does not. Most have dumbbells and a bench, which is enough for the full body session with dumbbells swapped in for the barbell. If there is nothing, the thirty minute home session works in a hotel room and needs no equipment at all. A resistance band weighs nothing and lives in your bag permanently.

**Steps.** Travel days are secretly the easiest step days you get. Airports, unfamiliar cities, walking to dinner. On a travel day you are often past 8,000 without trying, so on the days you are in transit, that is your win and you take it.

**Food.** Two scoops of whey in a shaker is your anchor meal anywhere on earth and it fits in a laptop bag. Beyond that, one rule: order protein first at every meal, then eat whatever else you want around it. You are not trying to eat well on the road. You are trying to hit protein and not spiral.

**The thing that actually goes wrong on trips** is not the food or the missed sessions. It is deciding that the whole week is a write off on day one, and then eating and drinking accordingly for six days. A trip where you hit protein, walked a lot and got two sessions in is a completely normal week by the standards of this document.

## Part seven. The only thing that actually breaks a routine

<Frame>
  ![It All Counts](/images/it-all-counts.png "It All Counts")
</Frame>

It is not travel. It is not a busy week. It is not a lack of time or equipment or the perfect plan.

The only thing that breaks a routine is the belief that anything less than the ideal version does not count.

Two sessions in a chaos week counts. Hitting protein on a travel day counts. A thirty minute home workout when you had planned a full session counts. It all counts, it all adds up, and the people who understand that are the ones still going three years later while everyone else is waiting for the right time.

The routine adapts. The commitment does not. That is the skill.

## The honest part

You now have the map from The Paved Path and the flexibility to actually walk it. Between them, that is clarity and ease, which are two of the three things that were missing.

And I will be straight with you again, because there is still a gap here that this document cannot close.

Everything above assumes you will do it. It assumes that on Sunday you will sit down for ten minutes and be honest about your week. It assumes that when a block gets destroyed on Tuesday, you move it that same hour instead of telling yourself you will sort it out later. It assumes that when the block hits on Wednesday evening and you are tired and nobody would ever know, you go anyway.

That is not information. That is accountability, and it is the one thing you were never wired for, because fitness was never built into your upbringing the way your career was. The people around you out here are not more disciplined than you. They simply had this one thing installed in them for years, and you did not.

That is the third part, and it is the one that decides everything.
